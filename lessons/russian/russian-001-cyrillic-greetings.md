# Russian 001 — Cyrillic Orientation and Greetings

**Estimated study time:** 25–35 minutes  
**Prerequisites:** None  
**Author:** Manus AI

## What you will be able to do

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to recognize that Russian is written in **Cyrillic**, read a small set of useful letters without relying on their English look-alikes, greet someone formally or informally, say goodbye, and introduce your name in a short exchange.

## Retrieval warm-up

There is no earlier lesson to recall. Instead, take one minute to establish the habit that will support later review. Cover the Russian text in the phrase table below. Read each English meaning, then uncover the Cyrillic and say it aloud. Do this twice: once now and once at the end of the lesson.

## 1. Cyrillic is a writing system, not a substitution cipher

Russian uses the Cyrillic alphabet. It has **33 letters**: 10 vowel letters, 21 consonant letters, and two signs that are not pronounced as independent sounds.[1] Some Cyrillic letters resemble Latin letters, but their shapes can be misleading. Learn the **sound** connected with a Russian letter rather than treating a familiar shape as English.

For this first lesson, accuracy matters more than speed. Transliteration is shown only as a temporary pronunciation aid. Read the Cyrillic first, then use the transliteration only to check yourself.

### Letters you can trust today

The following letters look and sound broadly familiar in the words used below.

| Cyrillic | Usual sound cue | Example |
|---|---|---|
| А а | **a** in *father* | мама — mother |
| К к | **k** in *kite* | как — how |
| М м | **m** in *mother* | мама — mother |
| О о | **o** when stressed; often reduced when unstressed | он — he |
| Т т | **t** in *stop* | ты — you, informal |

### Letters that look familiar but are false friends

| Cyrillic | Sound cue | Do not read it as |
|---|---|---|
| В в | **v** in *voice* | English *B* |
| Н н | **n** in *no* | English *H* |
| Р р | a rolled or tapped **r** | English *P* |
| С с | **s** in *see* | English *C* |
| У у | **oo** in *food* | English *Y* |
| Х х | a rough **kh**, like the sound in Scottish *loch* | English *X* |

> **Pronunciation habit:** Russian word stress affects vowels. A stressed vowel is clearer; an unstressed vowel may be shorter or change in quality.[1] At this stage, imitate whole phrases rather than trying to infer every sound from spelling alone.

### Three special letters to notice

You will meet these soon. Do not try to master them today.

| Letter | Practical first idea |
|---|---|
| Ё ё | usually sounds like **yo**; it is always stressed, though its two dots are often omitted in ordinary text.[1] |
| Ь | the **soft sign** has no sound of its own; it usually signals that the preceding consonant is soft. |
| Ы ы | a vowel that has no exact English equivalent; postpone fine detail until you have heard and practised it. |

## 2. Essential greetings

Russian choices depend on formality. Use **Здравствуйте** with people you do not know, in professional settings, or when you want a polite default. Use **Привет** with friends, peers, and people who have invited informal speech. In Russian, formal **you** is written **Вы**; informal singular **you** is **ты**.

| Russian | Temporary pronunciation aid | Precise meaning and use |
|---|---|---|
| Здравствуйте! | *zdrást-vuy-tye* | Hello; polite or formal. |
| Привет! | *pri-vyét* | Hi; informal. |
| Доброе утро! | *dó-bra-ye ú-tra* | Good morning. |
| Добрый день! | *dób-ryy dyen’* | Good afternoon / good day. |
| Добрый вечер! | *dób-ryy vyé-cher* | Good evening. |
| Пока! | *pa-ká* | Bye; informal. |
| До свидания! | *da svi-dá-ni-ya* | Goodbye; polite or formal. |
| Спасибо. | *spa-sí-ba* | Thank you. |

A short apostrophe in a pronunciation aid, as in *dyen’*, only reminds an English reader that the consonant is soft. It is not part of Russian spelling.

## 3. Say who you are

Two simple patterns are useful immediately.

| Russian | Temporary pronunciation aid | Translation |
|---|---|---|
| Меня зовут … | *mi-nyá za-vút …* | My name is … / I am called … |
| Я … | *ya …* | I am … |
| Как вас зовут? | *kak vas za-vút?* | What is your name? — polite/formal |
| Как тебя зовут? | *kak ti-byá za-vút?* | What is your name? — informal |
| Очень приятно. | *ó-chin’ pri-yát-na* | Nice to meet you. |

Notice the practical contrast:

- **Как вас зовут?** uses **вас**, the polite/formal form.
- **Как тебя зовут?** uses **тебя**, the informal singular form.

You do not need to analyze the grammar yet. Learn each question as a complete, useful pattern.

## 4. Worked dialogue

Read the following dialogue aloud twice. First use the pronunciation aid if needed. Then cover it and attempt the Cyrillic alone.

> **А:** Здравствуйте! Как вас зовут?  
> *Hello! What is your name?*  
>
> **Б:** Здравствуйте! Меня зовут Анна. А вас?  
> *Hello! My name is Anna. And you?*  
>
> **А:** Меня зовут Мира. Очень приятно.  
> *My name is Mira. Nice to meet you.*  
>
> **Б:** Очень приятно. До свидания!  
> *Nice to meet you. Goodbye!*

The name **Анна** is useful letter practice. Its letters are read approximately *Anna*: А = a, Н = n, Н = n, А = a. The shape **Н** is not English *H*.

## 5. Guided practice

Work through these without looking at the answers. Say every Russian answer aloud.

1. You meet a teacher for the first time. Choose the better greeting: **Привет** or **Здравствуйте**.
2. A close friend leaves. Choose the better farewell: **До свидания** or **Пока**.
3. Fill in the missing phrase: “_____ зовут Alex.”
4. Decide whether the question is formal or informal: **Как тебя зовут?**
5. Read these letters by sound, not English shape: **С О Н**.

## 6. Independent production exercise

Write and say a three-line introduction in Russian. Use your real name or an invented name. Keep the Cyrillic in your final version.

```text
[polite greeting]
Меня зовут [your name].
[polite farewell]
```

Then make an informal version for a friend. Replace the greeting and farewell appropriately. If you can, record yourself saying both versions and listen once for the distinction between your formal and informal choices.

## 7. Final self-check

You are ready for the next lesson if you can do all of the following without looking back:

1. Explain why **В** should not be read as English **B**.
2. Choose **Здравствуйте** rather than **Привет** for a first conversation with a stranger.
3. Say “My name is …” as **Меня зовут …**.
4. Recognize that **Как вас зовут?** is the polite/formal question.
5. Read **Анна** as a name with two **n** sounds, not with an English **H**.

If one item is difficult, repeat the phrase table and the dialogue later today. Short, accurate recall is more useful than rereading everything at once.

## Spoiler: answers and model response

1. **Здравствуйте** is the safer choice with a teacher you do not know.
2. **Пока** is appropriate for a close friend; **До свидания** is the polite/formal alternative.
3. **Меня**: **Меня зовут Alex.** A Russian spelling of a name can be learned later; using your own name after the phrase is fine for this exercise.
4. **Как тебя зовут?** is informal singular.
5. **С О Н** is approximately *son*. The first letter is the **s** sound, not English **C**.

A model polite introduction is:

> Здравствуйте!  
> Меня зовут Мира.  
> До свидания!
