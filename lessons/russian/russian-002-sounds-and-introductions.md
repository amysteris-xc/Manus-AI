## 1. Stress is part of a Russian word

Russian stress tells you which syllable receives the main prominence. It also affects vowel quality: stressed vowels are generally clearer, while some unstressed vowels are weaker or reduced.[1] For study, this lesson marks the stressed vowel with an acute accent. Ordinary Russian writing usually omits that accent.

| Study form | Ordinary spelling | Meaning | Temporary pronunciation aid |
|---|---|---|---|
| меня́ | меня | me / myself, in “my name is” | *mi-nyá* |
| зову́т | зовут | call, in “my name is” | *za-vút* |
| отку́да | откуда | from where | *at-kú-da* |
| Росси́и | России | of / from Russia, in `Я из России` | *ra-sí-i* |
| хорошо́ | хорошо | well, fine | *kha-ra-shó* |

Do not try to turn every written **о** into a perfect English *o*. In **хорошо́**, the final stressed **о** is clear; the earlier unstressed vowels are weaker. Listen-and-repeat practice will refine this over time. Your first goal is to put emphasis in the right place.

> **Study rule:** Read a new word once with its stress mark, then once without it. The mark is a learning aid, not a permanent part of normal spelling.

## 2. Four letter patterns that unlock more words

Russian letters carry sound information, but their position matters. These patterns are enough to make today’s introduction phrases easier to read.

### Е, Ё, Ю, and Я

At the start of a word, after a vowel, or after **ь** or **ъ**, the letters **е**, **ё**, **ю**, and **я** begin with a brief *y*-like sound. After many consonants, they also signal that the preceding consonant is soft. **Ё** is always stressed, although everyday text frequently prints it as **е**.[1]

| Letter | First practical cue | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Е е | often *ye* at a word start; often softens a preceding consonant | е́сли | if |
| Ё ё | *yo*; always stressed | ёлка | fir tree |
| Ю ю | *yu* at a word start | юг | south |
| Я я | *ya* at a word start; often softens a preceding consonant | я | I |

The purpose is not to guess every pronunciation from spelling. Notice the pattern, then connect it with audio and repeated speech. In **тебя́**, the **я** helps make the preceding **б** soft. In **меня́**, **я** does the same for **н**.

### The soft sign: ь

The soft sign **ь** has no independent sound. It usually tells you that the consonant before it is soft.[1]

| Word | Meaning | What to notice |
|---|---|---|
| день | day | **н** is soft before **ь** |
| семь | seven | **м** is soft before **ь** |
| очень | very | **н** is soft before **ь** |

Compare the spelling, not the exact sound, of **семья́** “family” and **се́мя** “seed.” The soft sign changes how the preceding consonant connects to the following letter. Do not pronounce **ь** as a separate vowel or consonant.

### High-value consonants

These consonants appear often in beginner vocabulary. Learn a dependable first approximation, then improve through audio.

| Cyrillic | First sound cue | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ж ж | *zh*, as in *measure* | уже́ | already |
| Ш ш | *sh*, as in *shop* | хорошо́ | well, fine |
| Ч ч | *ch*, as in *cheese* | ча́с | hour |
| Ц ц | *ts*, as in *tsar* | Росси́я | Russia |
| Й й | *y*, as in *boy* | мой | my |

## 3. A fuller introduction: name, origin, and a polite question

You already know **Меня зовут …**. Add one question and one answer:

| Russian | Temporary pronunciation aid | Precise meaning and use |
|---|---|---|
| Отку́да вы? | *at-kú-da vy?* | Where are you from? — polite/formal |
| Отку́да ты? | *at-kú-da ty?* | Where are you from? — informal singular |
| Я из … | *ya iz …* | I am from … / I come from … |
| Я из Росси́и. | *ya iz ra-sí-i* | I am from Russia. |
| О́чень прия́тно. | *ó-chin’ pri-yát-na* | Nice to meet you. |
| Спаси́бо, хорошо́. | *spa-sí-ba, kha-ra-shó* | Fine, thank you. |

The phrase **Я из …** is a useful memorized pattern. Do not attempt to build country names by rule yet; forms can change after **из**. Use a city or country name you have verified, or keep the blank until a later vocabulary lesson.

For introductions, preserve the formal/informal distinction learned in Russian 001:

- Use **вы** with a stranger, a client, a teacher, or anyone when you want a polite default.
- Use **ты** with a friend, child, or person who has made informal speech appropriate.

## 4. Worked dialogue

Read the dialogue once slowly with the stress marks. Then cover the English, read the Russian, and finally say both speakers’ lines aloud.

> **А:** Здра́вствуйте! Как вас зову́т?
> *Hello! What is your name?*
>
> **Б:** Меня́ зову́т А́нна. А вас?
> *My name is Anna. And you?*
>
> **А:** Меня́ зову́т Мира. О́чень прия́тно.
> *My name is Mira. Nice to meet you.*
>
> **Б:** Мне то́же. Отку́да вы?
> *Me too. Where are you from?*
>
> **А:** Я из Росси́и. А вы?
> *I am from Russia. And you?*

In regular spelling, the same dialogue normally appears without the learning accents:

```text
Здравствуйте! Как вас зовут?
Меня зовут Анна. А вас?
Меня зовут Мира. Очень приятно.
Мне тоже. Откуда вы?
Я из России. А вы?
```

The phrase **Мне тоже** is a compact answer meaning “Me too” in this context. Treat it as a complete response for now.

## 6. Independent production exercise

Write a four-to-six-line formal introduction. Use your real details only if you are comfortable; an invented name and place work equally well. Keep the stress marks for the first reading, then write a second version without them.

```text
Здравствуйте!
Меня́ зову́т [name].
Я из [place].
О́чень прия́тно.
До свида́ния!
```

Now record yourself reading both versions. On the second reading, do not look at the temporary pronunciation aids. Focus on the stressed syllables in **зову́т**, **отку́да**, and your place name if you know its stress.

## Spoiler: answers and model response

1. The stress is on the final syllable: хорошо́.
2. **ь**, the soft sign.
3. **Откуда вы?** is polite/formal.
4. **из**: **Я из России.**
5. **я** helps soften the preceding **б**.
6. **Ж** ≈ *zh*; **Ш** ≈ *sh*; **Ч** ≈ *ch*; **Ц** ≈ *ts*.
7. **Спасибо, хорошо.** means “Fine, thank you.” The usual Russian order is literally closer to “Thank you, fine.”[2]
8. From C 001, `%d` matches an `int`.

A model formal introduction is:

> Здравствуйте!
> Меня зовут Мира.
> Я из России.
> Очень приятно.
> До свидания!
